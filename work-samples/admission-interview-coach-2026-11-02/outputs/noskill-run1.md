Sam, here's my honest read: your interview was uneven. You gave one excellent answer and one strong one. Three others probably left Dana with doubts about whether you're senior yet. That's fixable, because the problem is a single habit and not a lack of experience.

## What went well

**The onboarding launch was your best moment, and it's a model answer.** You gave a baseline metric with a definition (31% activation, first invoice within seven days). You showed how you found the problem (twelve interviews, bank verification was the drop-off). You named a real constraint (eight weeks of work against a six-week regulatory deadline) and made a trade-off you owned (cutting multi-currency, knowing it would upset two enterprise prospects). You handled stakeholders ahead of time by telling sales first. Your result had a proper comparison (44% against a matched cohort, verification tickets down about half), and your reflection was specific (bring compliance in earlier). Dana said "Nice," and she meant it.

**The PCI answer was short and credible.** Moving from SAQ D to SAQ A with hosted fields is exactly what a payments company wants to hear, and you didn't over-explain it.

## The pattern to fix

Whenever a question had numbers and a timeline, you were specific and decisive. Whenever it was about **conflict, failure or judgment**, you went vague, said "we" and "everyone," and dodged taking a position. Senior PM interviews lean heavily on that second kind of question, and the next round will probably press harder there.

## Answer by answer

**1. "Disagreed with an engineering lead": the biggest concern.** Your story wasn't about engineering. When Dana asked again, you said you don't really have disagreements. To an interviewer, that sounds like you either avoid conflict or aren't close enough to the hard decisions to run into any. Neither is what they want from a senior PM. Dana's follow-up shows she noticed.

*Fix:* Pick a real disagreement where you held a position. Use this shape: what each of you wanted and why, what you did (data, a prototype, a compromise on scope), how it ended, and what you learned. You may already have one. Did engineering push back when you asked them to fit eight weeks of work into six? Don't make anything up. Find a true story and practise telling the tension clearly.

**2. Roadmap: you answered a different question.** Dana asked how you plan a *year*. You described sprint grooming, RICE scores on tickets, a Slack channel for requests, and 20% kept for tech debt. That's how you run a backlog, and it makes you sound mid-level.

*Fix:* Start with strategy and work down. Cover company and product goals, the 2–4 outcomes you'd commit to (for example, activation, payment volume, fewer losses), and the bets that serve them. Explain how discovery and data shape those bets and how you fit in non-negotiables like regulatory deadlines. Then say how you get leadership aligned and when you revisit the plan. Mention RICE only as one input for ranking.

**3. Failure: you blamed others, and your lesson pointed the wrong way.** You put the shelving down to the market, leadership changes and a VP leaving. You said you "didn't want to rock the boat." Your takeaway was "more resources." So the story became: it wasn't my fault, and I'd fix it by asking for more.

*Fix:* Own your part. Possible real lessons: you spent five months building before proving demand; there were no success or kill criteria agreed up front; when the new VP arrived, you didn't make a case using your beta data. What did those ~40 beta customers actually show? Use that. A stronger ending: "Now I set success criteria before building, test demand with something smaller than a five-month build, and when sponsors change I bring evidence and ask for a decision, not just go along with it."

**4. "Tell me about yourself": a résumé read aloud.** It was in date order, with no thread tying it together, and it ended on a generic line ("building products that users love"). It also hid your biggest selling point for this job: payments and regulated experience.

*Fix:* About 60–90 seconds with a clear thread. For example: "I'm a PM who works where onboarding meets payments in regulated B2B products. At Kestrel I lifted activation from 31% to 44% by rebuilding verification, and I led our PCI scope reduction. Before that I worked on checkout at an e-commerce company. I'm looking at Brightpay because…" Then give a real reason that's specific to Brightpay.

**5. Your question: a wasted chance.** "What does a typical day look like?" tells Dana nothing about how you think. Ask questions that show judgment, such as:
- "What would a great first six months look like for this role?"
- "How do product and compliance work together here? Early in discovery, or as a review at the end?" (This ties back to your own onboarding lesson.)
- "What's the hardest trade-off the team is facing right now?"

1. **Prepare a true engineering-disagreement story** with real tension and a clear position you took. Say it out loud until it takes under two minutes.
2. **Rebuild the failure story** around what you'd do differently, not what others did.
3. **Practise a strategy-first roadmap answer**, ideally using a realistic Brightpay scenario. Later rounds often include a product or strategy case.
4. **Rewrite your intro** to lead with payments, regulated work and the 31→44% result, and close with a specific reason for choosing Brightpay. Research what they build so that reason holds up.
5. **Prepare 3–4 sharp questions** for each interviewer.
6. **Draft 5–6 stories** (conflict, failure, influence without authority, a hard prioritisation call, a decision with incomplete data) and give each one the detail your onboarding answer had: numbers, trade-offs, what *you* did, what you learned.

One drill to run before you answer anything: ask yourself "what did *I* decide, and what did it cost?" Your onboarding answer already does that. Make every answer do the same.
