# High Output Management
*Andy Grove, 1983*

---

## The Core Principle

A manager's output = output of their team + output of other teams they influence.

Management is not status. It is a production function. Your job is to maximise the output of the system, not to do the work yourself.

---

## Leverage — Where to Spend Your Time

**Leverage = Output produced / Time spent**

Every hour you have can be invested at high or low leverage. Choose deliberately.

### High-leverage activities

**Training** — An hour teaching 10 people multiplies across every future task they do. Highest ROI of any management activity.

**Decisions that unblock others** — When your decision is the bottleneck, delayed decisions multiply downstream delays. Make them faster than feels comfortable.

**Information sharing** — Context you hold that others lack is a bottleneck you may not notice. Share it proactively.

**Motivation and recognition** — Peak performance requires meaning, not just direction. Recognition costs nothing and compounds.

### Low-leverage traps

**Doing the work yourself** — If someone on your team can do it at 70% of your quality, delegate it. Your job is enabling output, not performing it.

**Being in meetings you don't change** — If the meeting would happen the same way without you, you do not need to be there.

**Over-reviewing work** — Match review depth to risk and the person's task-relevant maturity (see below). Over-review is micromanagement with extra steps.

**Meddling tells:** People wait for your approval on things they should own. You are always the last step. Your team's work is good but they don't believe you think so.

**The fix:** Delegate. Then do not take it back.

## The 1:1

The most important ritual in a manager's week. Not a status update — an information exchange and a coaching session.

**The 1:1 belongs to the report, not the manager.** Their agenda drives the conversation.

**Structure:**
1. What is going well (report leads)
2. Challenges and blockers (report leads)
3. Manager asks clarifying questions — does not lecture
4. Development or growth discussion
5. Manager shares relevant context the report needs

**Frequency:** Weekly is the baseline. Fortnightly for senior or highly independent people. More frequent for anyone new to a task or struggling.

**Prep discipline:** The manager comes prepared with one specific piece of feedback (positive or developmental) and one question they genuinely do not know the answer to. Vague feedback ("you're doing great") has zero value.

**Signal that your 1:1s are broken:** You mostly talk. The report mostly listens. Fix this.

## OKRs

Andy Grove invented OKRs at Intel. John Doerr brought them to Google. The framework is simple; the discipline is hard.

### Structure

**Objective** — What do we want to achieve? Qualitative, inspirational, time-bound (quarterly).

**Key Results** — How will we know we achieved it? Quantitative, measurable, 3–5 per objective.

### What good looks like

**Objective:** Become the most trusted resource for icare scheme design in the programme.

**Key Results:**
1. Produce 3 evidence-graded scheme analyses rated "decision-ready" by stakeholders
2. Reduce time from discovery question to structured output from 5 days to 2 days
3. Have multi-actor-research playbook adopted by 2 other team members

### What bad looks like

**Objective:** Improve the work.
**Key Results:** Do better research. Write more clearly. Help the team.

Problems: objective is vague, key results are not measurable, no target to aim for.

### Principles

**Stretch:** 70% achievement = success. 100% means you aimed too low. OKRs are not a to-do list.

**Cascade:** Team OKRs support programme OKRs. Individual OKRs support team OKRs.

**Separate from performance review:** OKRs are for focus and alignment, not for bonus calculation. Linking them to pay causes sandbagging.

**Cadence:** Set quarterly. Check in weekly. Score at the end (0.0 to 1.0). Do not change OKRs mid-quarter unless something fundamental shifts.

## Task-Relevant Maturity (TRM)

The most underused idea in management. The right management style depends not on the person's overall seniority, but on their experience with *this specific task*.

| TRM Level | What it looks like | Right management style |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Low | New to this task; uncertain; risk of failure without support | Structured — clear instructions, defined process, frequent check-ins, more directive |
| Medium | Some experience; can do basics independently; needs coaching for harder problems | Coaching — ask more than tell, check results not process, problem-solve together |
| High | Expert at this task; knows more than you might; self-directed | Delegation — set the objective and get out of the way; available when needed |

**Wrong match = predictable failure:**

High TRM + Directive = demotivation ("you're micromanaging me")
Low TRM + Delegation = failure ("nobody told me what to do")

**TRM is task-specific, not person-specific.** A principal service designer with High TRM on research has Low TRM when taking on a new stakeholder relationship they have never managed. Manage accordingly.

**Reassess TRM when:** new tasks or responsibilities, new environment, declining performance, or the person explicitly asks for more or less support.

## Influencing Without Authority

Grove's leverage framework applies directly to influence work — the domain where you produce output through relationships and information, not through line management.

High-leverage moves for influence without authority:
- Share analysis that makes others' decisions easier (information leverage)
- Train or coach peers in your methods (training leverage)
- Facilitate decisions that are stuck (unblocking leverage)
- Make your outputs so reliable that others depend on them (credibility leverage)

The question to ask before any influencing activity: "Is this the highest-leverage intervention available to me right now, or am I just doing the most visible thing?"

## Quick Diagnostics

| Symptom | Likely cause | Grove's fix |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Team is slow and blockers persist | Your decisions are the bottleneck | Decide faster; delegate more decision authority |
| Good people are disengaged | High TRM + directive management | Pull back; set outcomes not process |
| Junior people keep failing quietly | Low TRM + delegated management | Structure more; check in more often |
| You are always in the critical path | Low leverage activity mix | Audit your week; shift toward training and unblocking |
| Your 1:1s feel like status updates | Report's agenda not driving | Reset: explicitly hand the meeting to them |
