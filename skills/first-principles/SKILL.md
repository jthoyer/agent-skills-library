# First-Principles Thinking

Strip a problem back to what is objectively true, then rebuild from there — ignoring convention, habit, and institutional inertia.

---

## The Core Method

Three steps, applied in order:

**1. Identify the assumptions**
List everything the current approach takes for granted. Which of these are genuine constraints (legislative, physical, financial) and which are inherited habits or institutional comfort?

**2. Establish the fundamental truths**
What is actually, objectively true about this problem? What does the legislation actually require? What does the participant actually need? What would have to be true for any solution to work?

**3. Rebuild from the truths**
Given only what is genuinely true — not what is conventional — what would you design? What looks different? What would you stop doing?

---

## The 15 First-Principles Prompts

### Strip to fundamentals

**1. What are the physics of this problem?**
Remove opinions, preferences, and history. What is objectively real?
*Use when:* You need to separate what is actually true from what you believe is true.

**2. If I could not rely on existing assumptions, how would I solve this?**
Assumptions are invisible constraints. This prompt forces fresh framing.
*Use when:* You are stuck in "the way things are done."

**3. What are the problem's fundamental components?**
Break it into irreducible elements. What are the atoms of this issue?
*Use when:* The problem feels overwhelming or shapeless.

### Unconstrained vision

**4. What would the optimal solution look like if legacy systems did not exist?**
Imagine the purpose before the infrastructure. What are you actually trying to achieve?
*Use when:* Systems and processes have accumulated around an original intent that has since been obscured.

**5. If I had to cut 90% of this, what would remain?**
Brutal prioritisation. What is genuinely essential?
*Use when:* Too many features, processes, or requirements are competing for attention.

### Failure modes

**6. If this failed completely, what would be the root cause?**
Pre-mortem thinking. Engineer success from the failure scenario.
*Use when:* Planning a major commitment or assessing risk before launch.

**7. What would a solution look like if I ignored institutional norms?**
Bypass inherited practice entirely.
*Use when:* Building something genuinely new, or diagnosing why current designs produce poor outcomes.

**8. What part of this is actually impossible, and what part just feels impossible?**
Most limits are institutional or emotional, not physical. Separate real constraints from inherited fear.
*Use when:* Hesitating on a decision or feeling stuck without a clear reason.

### Breakthrough

**9. What is the minimum viable breakthrough?**
Not minimum viable product — minimum viable *change* that shifts the system.
*Use when:* Resources are constrained but change is needed.

**10. If I restarted this programme today, knowing what I know now, what would I design?**
Clean-slate thinking. Sunk costs are irrelevant.
*Use when:* You have accumulated design debt, process complexity, or legacy decisions that no longer serve the purpose.

### Hidden constraints

**11. What are the hidden constraints I am not questioning?**
Most problems have fake walls alongside the real ones. Find and challenge them.
*Use when:* Progress has plateaued or something that should be solvable keeps not being solved.

**12. How would I solve this if I only cared about the participant's outcome, not the system's convenience?**
Remove institutional friction from problem solving. What does the person in the scheme actually need?
*Use when:* Organisational dynamics or system constraints are producing participant-unfriendly designs.

### Scale and leverage

**13. If I had to achieve this twice as fast, what would I stop doing?**
Time compression reveals what is genuinely necessary.
*Use when:* Timelines are driving decisions about what is essential.

**14. Which single part of this solution creates the most leverage?**
Build the part that changes everything else first.
*Use when:* Deciding where to focus limited capacity.

**15. What does the legislation actually require — versus what has been built around it?**
Separate the statutory mandate from the institutional accretion that has grown up around it.
*Use when:* Scheme design or process feels over-engineered relative to its legal purpose.

## Application to icare / Service Design

First-principles thinking is particularly useful when:

- A scheme has accumulated process complexity that obscures what participants actually need
- A service expectation or policy exists but its original rationale has been lost
- A Navigator/FINEOS migration requires deciding what the system must do (fundamental) vs what it currently does (historical)
- A cross-functional team has inherited assumptions from different functions that are in conflict

**Grounding questions for icare work:**
- What does the legislation actually mandate for this participant, stripped of operational interpretation?
- If a participant came to us today with no knowledge of our processes, what would they need from us?
- Which of our current service steps exist because of regulation, and which exist because of institutional habit?
- What would we design if our only constraint was the Act — not the legacy system, not the current org structure?

---

## Output Structure

When applying first-principles to a problem:

1. Problem statement — specific and clarified
2. Assumptions identified — the full list, graded (genuine constraint vs inherited habit)
3. Fundamental truths — what is objectively true
4. Analysis — relevant prompts applied
5. Insights — what looks different when convention is stripped away
6. Recommendations — what to do next, grounded in the truths
7. Open questions — what still needs investigation before acting
