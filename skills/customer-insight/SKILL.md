# Customer Insight
## Jobs to Be Done + Value Proposition Canvas

Two frameworks. One question: *what does the customer actually need, and does your offering fit?*

---

## Which Framework to Use

| You need to... | Use |
|---------------|-----|
| Understand *why* customers behave the way they do | JTBD |
| Design discovery interview questions | JTBD |
| Map the competitive landscape from the customer's perspective | JTBD |
| Diagnose adoption failure or churn | JTBD |
| Map customer jobs, pains, and gains to your offering | VPC |
| Achieve or validate product-market fit | VPC |
| Prioritise features for an MVP | VPC |
| Reposition an offering for a new segment | VPC |
| Do both — deep discovery then structured mapping | Run JTBD first, feed findings into VPC |

Use JTBD to *discover*. Use VPC to *design and map*. They are not alternatives — they are sequential.

## Part 1: Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

*Based on Clayton Christensen's framework from "Competing Against Luck" (2016).*

### Core Principle

Customers don't buy products — they **hire** them to make progress in their lives.

> **Job to Be Done** = the progress a customer wants to make in specific circumstances.

Three elements that define a job:
- **Progress** — not a goal or task, but movement from one state to a better one
- **Circumstances** — context determines the job, not demographics
- **Hire/Fire** — the customer actively chooses what best accomplishes the job

---

### The Three Dimensions of Every Job

Always define a job across all three dimensions — functional alone is incomplete.

| Dimension | Question | Example |
|-----------|----------|---------|
| Functional | What does the customer need to do? | Get through the commute without feeling hungry |
| Emotional | How do they want to feel? | A small treat, without guilt |
| Social | How do they want to be perceived? | A sensible choice, not indulgent |

---

### The Job Statement

**Format:** "When [circumstances], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome]."

Rules:
- Keep the job statement entirely about the customer — leave your product or solution out
- Circumstances matter more than customer attributes
- Jobs are stable over time; solutions change, jobs don't

---

### Forces of Progress

Four forces determine whether a customer switches to a new solution:

```
Push (frustration with current situation)
Pull (attraction of the new solution)
─────────────────────────────────────────  ← Switch happens when Push + Pull > Habit + Anxiety
Habit (comfort with current behaviour)
Anxiety (fear of the new)
```

**Most product teams only increase Pull and ignore Habit and Anxiety. This is why good products fail to get adopted.**

Interventions by force:
- **Reduce Anxiety:** free trials, money-back guarantees, social proof, migration support
- **Reduce Habit:** effortless migration, one-click import, familiar interface patterns
- **Increase Push:** content that names the frustration ("still doing X manually?")
- **Increase Pull:** concrete demonstrations of the new outcome

---

### Non-Obvious Competition

True competition = everything a customer can hire for the same job.

Define competition by the job, not the product category. A milkshake competes with bananas, podcasts, and conversation. Netflix competes with sleep.

**Mapping prompt:** "List every alternative the customer could hire to accomplish [the job], including doing nothing."

Non-consumption (doing nothing) is almost always the biggest competitor.

---

### Customer Discovery Interviews

Reconstruct the purchase timeline through specific events, rather than asking customers what they want — most customers can't articulate it.

**Interview arc:**

| Stage | Sample questions |
|-------|-----------------|
| First thought | "When did you first think about looking for a solution? What was happening?" |
| Search | "What alternatives did you look at? What eliminated options?" |
| Purchase | "Where were you when you decided? What finally convinced you? What worried you?" |
| Usage | "Is it doing what you expected? What surprised you? What's still missing?" |

**Signals of undiscovered jobs:** workarounds, non-consumption, compensating behaviours, negative emotions with current solutions.

**Rule:** Use exact customer language from interviews in product copy and positioning. Transcribe their words rather than translating them.

---

### Big Hire vs Little Hire

| | Big Hire | Little Hire |
|--|---------|------------|
| What | The purchase/signup decision | The decision to use it in the moment |
| Frequency | Once | Repeatedly |
| Driven by | Marketing, onboarding, first impression | Product quality, UX, ongoing value |
| Failure looks like | Low conversion | High churn; bought but not used |

**Retention problems are almost always Little Hire failures, not Big Hire failures.**

---

### Common JTBD Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Defining jobs around your product | Write the job statement without mentioning your product |
| Ignoring emotional and social dimensions | Complete all three dimensions every time |
| Confusing jobs with tasks | Tasks are too specific; jobs describe progress in circumstances |
| Only increasing Pull | Map all four forces; design for Anxiety and Habit reduction |
| Asking "what do you want?" | Use timeline-based interviews that reconstruct real decisions |
| Defining competition by category | Map all hires for the same job, including non-consumption |

## Part 2: Value Proposition Canvas (VPC)

*Based on Osterwalder et al., "Value Proposition Design" (2014), Strategyzer.*

### Core Principle

80% of new products fail not because they lack functionality, but because they miss what customers actually need. The VPC creates explicit FIT between what you offer and what customers value.

---

### The Canvas Structure

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│           VALUE MAP (square)     CUSTOMER PROFILE (circle)   │
│                                                              │
│   ┌──────────────────┐           ┌──────────────────┐       │
│   │ Products &       │           │ Customer Jobs    │       │
│   │ Services         │  ← FIT →  │ Pains            │       │
│   │ Pain Relievers   │           │ Gains            │       │
│   │ Gain Creators    │           │                  │       │
│   └──────────────────┘           └──────────────────┘       │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

FIT is achieved when your value map addresses the most important jobs, relieves the most significant pains, and creates the most valued gains.

---

### Customer Profile

**Customer Jobs** — what the customer is trying to get done (functional, emotional, social). These are the JTBD inputs.

**Customer Pains** — obstacles, risks, and frustrations that prevent job completion or make it worse than it needs to be. Rank by severity.

**Customer Gains** — outcomes and benefits the customer wants. Distinguish:
- Required gains (basic expectations — if absent, it fails)
- Expected gains (what good looks like)
- Desired gains (would love it but not expecting it)
- Unexpected gains (would delight them; they'd never think to ask)

---

### Value Map

**Products and Services** — the offering itself. List everything the customer receives.

**Pain Relievers** — how your offering reduces or eliminates specific pains. Map each reliever explicitly to a pain.

**Gain Creators** — how your offering produces outcomes the customer wants. Map each creator explicitly to a gain.

**Discipline:** List only what demonstrably addresses a named pain or creates a named gain — not every feature.

---

### Achieving FIT

Three levels of fit — work through them in order:

| Level | Question | How |
|-------|----------|-----|
| Problem-solution fit | Does your offering address real jobs, pains, and gains? | Customer discovery (JTBD interviews) |
| Product-market fit | Do customers value it enough to choose it over alternatives? | Validated demand — people buy or commit |
| Business model fit | Can you deliver the value proposition sustainably? | Revenue, cost, and channel validation |

---

### FIT Gaps — Common Failure Patterns

| Pattern | Diagnosis |
|---------|-----------|
| Low conversion but strong interest | Value map addresses the wrong gains (desired, not required) |
| High conversion, high churn | Value map addresses the Big Hire but not the Little Hire (JTBD) |
| "Interesting but not for me" response | Customer profile is wrong — different segment has a different job |
| Feature requests that surprise the team | Pain relievers are addressing assumed pains, not actual ones |
| Commoditisation pressure | Gain creators match competitors; no differentiated FIT |

---

### VPC Prompts

Use these to run the framework:

**Discovery:** "Help me build a VPC for [offering]. My target customer is [description]. Walk me through the customer profile first, then the value map, then assess FIT."

**Diagnosis:** "My product isn't selling. Use the VPC to diagnose why. Offering: [description]. Target: [customer]. Identify the FIT gaps."

**Pivot:** "I'm moving to a new customer segment. Use the VPC to map [segment] and design a value proposition that fits their jobs, pains, and gains."

**MVP prioritisation:** "I have these potential features: [list]. Use the VPC to identify which address the most important pains and gains."

---

## Part 3: Running Both Together

### The Sequence

```
JTBD Discovery ──> Customer Profile (VPC) ──> Value Map (VPC) ──> FIT Assessment
     ↑                                                                    │
     └────────────────── Iterate if FIT gaps found ────────────────────┘
```

1. Run JTBD discovery interviews (10+ interviews minimum)
2. Extract the job statement, forces, and competitive landscape
3. Build the Customer Profile from interview findings — jobs, pains, gains grounded in evidence
4. Design the Value Map explicitly against the profile
5. Assess FIT — which pains are unaddressed? Which gains are uncreated?
6. Iterate — either adjust the offering or revisit the customer segment

### Red Flags That Require Going Back to JTBD

- The customer profile is based on assumptions, not interviews
- The jobs section lists tasks ("track my spending") not progress ("feel in control of my money")
- Pain severity rankings are guessed, not evidenced
- Gain creators address expected or desired gains but not required ones
