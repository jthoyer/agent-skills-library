# Made to Stick
*Chip Heath & Dan Heath, 2006*

Sticky ideas are not born — they are made. The SUCCESs framework identifies why some ideas survive and others die, and how to engineer for the difference.

---

## The Curse of Knowledge

The single greatest barrier to effective communication.

Once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it. You skip context that feels obvious. You use jargon. You abstract rather than concrete. You explain at the level of the knower, not the recipient.

**The fix:** Test every important message with someone outside your context. If they cannot restate the core in their own words, you have not communicated it yet.

---

## The SUCCESs Framework

Six principles. Not a checklist — a toolkit. The stickiest ideas use most of them; no single idea needs all six.

### Simple

Find the core of the idea and express it compactly. Simple ≠ dumbed down. It means ruthless prioritisation of the one thing that matters most.

**Commander's Intent:** If your audience remembers only one thing from this communication, what must it be? Lead with that. Add detail in decreasing order of importance.

**The test:** Can you state the core message in one sentence, without jargon?

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| "AI-powered customer engagement platform with omnichannel capabilities" | "Talk to all your customers in one place" |
| "Cost drivers across the scheme portfolio are volatile and poorly understood" | "We do not know what is driving costs — and that is the risk" |

---

### Unexpected

Break a pattern to get attention. Open a gap in knowledge to hold it.

**Surprise:** Identify the counterintuitive implication of your core message and lead with it. Not manufactured shock — genuine inversion of what the audience expects.

**Curiosity gap:** State that there is something they do not know yet. Then delay the answer long enough for the gap to feel real. Mystery structures work: problem first, resolution later.

**The test:** Would this sentence make someone stop scrolling?

---

### Concrete

Anchor abstract ideas in sensory, specific language.

Abstract: "Participants experience significant delays in claim resolution."
Concrete: "A participant with silicosis waits an average of 14 months from diagnosis to receiving their first support payment."

**Rule:** Replace every abstraction with a specific, observable example. Numbers become sticky when they are human-scale. "The policy covers 1.4 million workers" is abstract. "That's every person who commutes on the Sydney Trains network, twice" is concrete.

---

### Credible

Give the audience a reason to believe.

Sources of credibility:
- **Authority** — expert, institution, data source
- **Anti-authority** — the person with most to lose who still endorses it
- **Specifics** — precise numbers are more credible than round numbers ("saves 14 minutes" not "saves time")
- **Sinatra Test** — if it can survive the hardest case, it can survive anything ("if this works for DDC participants, it works everywhere")
- **Testable** — can the audience verify it themselves?

---

### Emotional

People act on feelings. Logic earns agreement; emotion earns action.

**One person, not statistics:** "1 in 5 workers with a psychological injury returns to work" is a fact. "David was a builder for 22 years. After his psychological injury, he couldn't imagine returning. He's now back at 60% capacity." is a story that moves people.

**Identity appeals:** Connect to how the audience sees themselves, or wants to be seen. "People like us do things like this."

**The test:** Does this message make someone feel something — not just think something?

---

### Stories

Narrative is how humans understand cause and effect. Facts inform; stories prepare people to act.

Three story structures that work universally:

| Type | Structure | Use when |
|------|-----------|----------|
| Challenge | Protagonist overcomes an obstacle | Showing change is possible |
| Connection | Two people bridge a divide | Building empathy across groups |
| Creativity | Someone finds an unexpected solution | Proposing a new approach |

**For icare outputs:** Participant stories are the most powerful credibility and empathy tool available. One specific, real (or representative) participant journey — told with concrete detail, without sentimentality — is worth more than a hundred slides of scheme data.

## Sticky Messaging Audit

Rate any communication before it goes out:

| Principle | Question | Score (1–10) |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| Simple | Is there one clear core message? | |
| Unexpected | Does it break a pattern or open a curiosity gap? | |
| Concrete | Can you picture it? Are there specific details? | |
| Credible | Why should someone believe this? | |
| Emotional | Does it make you feel something? | |
| Stories | Is there a narrative with a character? | |

**50–60:** Extremely sticky. **35–49:** Strong. **20–34:** Forgettable. **Below 20:** Needs fundamental rework.

## Application to icare Executive Communications

| Element | Weak version | Sticky version |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| Simple | "The scheme has multiple cost drivers requiring multi-factorial analysis" | "We don't know what's driving costs. That's the problem we're solving." |
| Unexpected | "Participants experience delays" | "Most participants don't know their claim has stalled. They think they're still in the queue." |
| Concrete | "Long-tail claims are expensive" | "The 300 workers in our longest-running claims account for more spend than the next 3,000 combined." |
| Credible | "Research suggests..." | "The Commission's 2023 review found..." |
| Emotional | "Poor service outcomes affect participant wellbeing" | "One in three DDC participants dies before their claim is resolved." |
| Stories | Statistics about dust diseases | The story of one participant — their industry, their diagnosis, their wait. |

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| Burying the lead | Core message is lost in context-setting | State the core first; context supports it |
| Too abstract | Nothing to remember or picture | Replace every abstraction with a specific example |
| Feature listing | No emotional connection | Wrap in a story or a concrete consequence |
| Jargon | Curse of Knowledge — you have forgotten what it's like not to know | Test with an outsider |
| Round numbers | Imprecise numbers feel made up | "14 months" is more credible than "over a year" |
