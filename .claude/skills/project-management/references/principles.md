## Why Principles Before Rituals

The most common failure in project management is selecting rituals before understanding what those rituals need to achieve. A stand-up copied from another team's playbook, running in a context it wasn't designed for, quickly becomes a chore. Principles first forces the question: *what does good coordination actually need to produce on this project?*

The fundamental answer is: **the right people have the right information at the right time to make good decisions without surprises.** Every ritual and communication should trace back to this.

## The Seven Principles — Extended Rationale

### 1. Information should flow, not pool

On small teams, the lead is often the node through which all context passes. This creates fragility: if the lead is absent, the team loses situational awareness. It also creates bottlenecks in decision-making and slows down the senior and junior's ability to contribute fully.

Information flowing means:
- The senior can brief a stakeholder without the lead present
- The junior understands the project goal well enough to explain it simply
- Blockers surface quickly, not at the weekly meeting

**Practice:** At the start of each week, test whether the senior could run the project for 48 hours without the lead. If not, something is being held too tightly.

---

### 2. Rituals serve the work, not the other way around

The ritual stack should be the minimum set needed to keep the project coordinated. Over-ritualling — too many meetings, too many touchpoints — is as harmful as under-ritualling. It fragments deep work time, trains people to stop engaging, and signals distrust.

**First-principles check:** If I had to cut half the rituals, which would survive? Start with those.

**Signs you're over-ritualled:**
- People are attending but not contributing
- The same topics come up in multiple meetings
- The retro has become a venting session
- The stand-up regularly runs over 20 minutes

---

### 3. Distinguish alignment from working sessions

Alignment meetings (stand-ups, kick-offs, stakeholder updates) have a different purpose than working sessions (workshops, synthesis sessions, design crits). Mixing them produces bad versions of both.

An alignment meeting that drifts into problem-solving means the stand-up runs 45 minutes. A working session that starts with status updates means the real work gets 20 minutes.

**Practice:** State the meeting type at the start. "This is an alignment check — we're not solving anything today, just making sure we're coordinated."

---

### 4. The junior's development is a project deliverable

On a three-person team, the junior's growth directly affects the team's capacity and resilience. A junior who is only assigned tasks — never given stretch, never briefed before presentations, never given specific feedback — grows slowly and stays dependent.

**This matters for the lead because:**
- A more capable junior reduces the lead's load over the course of the project
- Being seen to develop others is part of being a strong project lead
- It's connected to curriculum area 14 (Developing and Supporting Your Team)

**Practice:** Each fortnight, the lead should be able to name one specific thing the junior has done that they couldn't do at the start of the project.

---

### 5. Stakeholders should never be surprised

A surprise for a stakeholder — a missed milestone, an emerging risk, a change in direction — is almost always a communication failure. The issue itself may have been unavoidable. The surprise wasn't.

Surprise erodes trust faster than problems do. A stakeholder who is kept informed through difficulties maintains confidence. A stakeholder who discovers a problem through a third party loses confidence in the team's judgement.

**Practice:** Before sending any status update, ask: "Is there anything in here that would surprise the reader?" If yes, flag it explicitly rather than burying it.

---

### 6. Decisions must be documented, not just discussed

The most common source of rework and misalignment in service design projects is undocumented decisions. People remember different things. Context that seemed obvious at the time becomes unclear three weeks later. New stakeholders join without access to the rationale.

Decision documentation doesn't need to be elaborate. A single line in a shared doc — *"14 Feb: Agreed to narrow scope to registered users only. Rationale: resource constraint. Owner: Lead."* — prevents enormous amounts of confusion.

**Practice:** Designate a "decisions log" at project kick-off. A running doc that captures what was decided, why, and by whom. Reference it in status updates.

---

### 7. Close the loop explicitly

In stakeholder-rich environments, questions and concerns get raised and then fall through the cracks. The stakeholder assumes it's being handled. The team assumes the stakeholder forgot. Six weeks later, it surfaces as an issue.

Closing the loop means: when a question or concern is raised, it gets logged, actioned, and the response is confirmed with the person who raised it. Not just answered — confirmed.

**Practice:** At the end of every stakeholder meeting, the lead reads back any open items: "You raised X — we'll come back to you on that by [date]." Then do it.

## On the Escalation Protocol

Escalation is one of the most mismanaged aspects of small team project work. Two failure modes are common:

**Under-escalation:** The team holds a problem internally, hoping it resolves, until it becomes a crisis. By the time the manager is informed, options are limited and trust is damaged.

**Over-escalation:** Every issue goes upward. The manager becomes the team's decision-maker by default. The lead loses credibility as someone who can handle complexity independently.

The right protocol:
- Escalate early, when options still exist
- Arrive with options and a recommendation, not an open question
- Be clear about what you need: a decision, a resource, an introduction, awareness only
- Document what was decided and who owns it before leaving the meeting

**The test:** A good escalation leaves the manager thinking "I'm glad they told me early." A poor escalation leaves them thinking "Why didn't I hear about this sooner?" or "Why are they asking me to solve this?"
