# Positioning
*April Dunford's 5+1 Framework — "Obviously Awesome" (2019)*

Positioning is the context you set that determines how customers perceive your offering. Change the context without changing the product, and the same thing can succeed or fail.

---

## When Positioning Is the Problem

Weak positioning shows up as:

| Symptom | What it looks like |
|---------|-------------------|
| "What is it?" confusion | Prospects ask "So are you like X?" 15 minutes into a demo |
| Wrong price comparisons | "I love it but it's too expensive" — they're comparing to the wrong category |
| Feature gap requests | Prospects ask for features irrelevant to your actual value |
| High churn | Customers leave saying "I thought it would do X" |
| Long sales cycles | It takes forever to explain why this matters |

If 3+ of these are present, the problem is positioning, not the product.

---

## The 5+1 Components

Work through these in order — each depends on the previous.

### 1. Competitive Alternatives

**Question:** What would customers do if your solution did not exist?

This is rarely a named direct competitor. It is usually:
- The status quo ("doing nothing", "living with the pain")
- A manual workaround (spreadsheets, email, memory)
- An in-house solution built years ago
- A different category of tool solving an adjacent problem

**Warning — the Phantom Competitor fallacy:** Do not position against Salesforce if customers are actually using spreadsheets. You will lose because you are fighting the wrong battle.

List your real alternatives:
1. _______________
2. _______________
3. _______________

### 2. Unique Attributes

**Question:** What do you have that the alternatives from step 1 genuinely lack?

Rules:
- Must compare to the actual alternatives named above, not imagined competitors
- Must be factual and provable — "easy to use" does not count without data
- If a competitor also has it, it is not a unique attribute

|-----------|--------------------------------|
| | |

### 3. Value (and Proof)

**Question:** What business outcome do those attributes enable?

Engineers describe attributes. Buyers care about outcomes. The translation layer is your value.

| Unique attribute | → | Value to buyer | Proof |
|-----------------|---|---------------|-------|
| [feature] | → | [business outcome] | [data / case study] |

---

### 4. Target Market Characteristics

**Question:** Who cares disproportionately about this value?

Bad: "We target mid-sized organisations."
Good: "We target mid-sized organisations currently undergoing a system migration who need the transition to preserve participant experience."

What situation makes this value urgent? What event triggers the decision?

---

### 5. Market Category

**Question:** What frame of reference makes your unique attributes look like must-have features?

The category you choose determines:
- Who you are compared against
- What budget you are competing for
- What the buyer already expects

| Category option | Competitive set | Your position |
|-----------------|----------------|---------------|
| [A] | [Competitors] | Strong / Weak |
| [B] | [Competitors] | Strong / Weak |

Choose the category where your unique attributes = the category's defining requirements.

---

### +1. Relevant Trend (optional)

**Question:** What external trend makes this solution urgent right now?

Rules: the trend must connect directly to your value. Do not attach to irrelevant trends — buyers notice cynicism immediately. The trend creates urgency; it does not create the positioning.

---

## Three Positioning Styles

**Head-to-head:** Position directly against a known market leader. Requires a genuine, provable differentiation. High risk, high reward.

**Big fish, small pond (recommended for most B2B and service contexts):** Pick a sub-segment where your attributes are the defining requirements. Own that segment before expanding. This is usually the right starting point.

**Category creation:** Name a new category you define. Requires significant market education investment. Appropriate only when the existing categories are genuinely wrong frames, not just inconvenient ones.

## Applying This to Service Design Practice Positioning

If the context is positioning a service design function, a practice, or a PM portfolio rather than a commercial product:

- **Competitive alternatives** = what the organisation was doing before this function existed (ad hoc design, IT-led delivery, consultant reports)
- **Unique attributes** = what this function does that those alternatives cannot (participant-grounded evidence, cross-scheme synthesis, influencing without authority)
- **Value** = what changes as a result (better scheme outcomes, faster decisions, fewer redesigns)
- **Target market** = who in the organisation cares most about those outcomes (scheme directors, programme leads, icare executive)
- **Category** = how to describe the function in language the organisation uses and values
