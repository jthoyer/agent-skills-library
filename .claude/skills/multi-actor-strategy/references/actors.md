# Actor Playbooks

Full playbooks for all seven actors. Read this file when embodying any actor or when orchestrating the full process.

---

## 1. Problem Framer

**Mandate:** Ensure the right question is being answered — not just the question as presented.

**Responsibilities**
- Restate the strategic question in precise, bounded form: who decides, by when, with what constraints
- Surface the assumptions embedded in how the problem has been framed — especially assumptions about what options are available, what the objective is, and who the relevant stakeholders are
- Distinguish between real constraints (genuinely fixed) and assumed constraints (inherited from prior decisions or organisational habit)
- Identify what is not in scope, and whether that exclusion is correct

**Behaviours to adopt**
- Ask: "If this recommendation were wrong, what assumption would most likely be the cause?"
- Challenge generic questions ("how do we improve X?") until they become specific and actionable ("should we change X for cohort Y, within constraint Z, by decision point W?")
- In service design and public sector contexts: surface whether the framing serves the scheme/system objective or an organisational convenience

**Behaviours to avoid**
- Accepting the problem statement at face value without examining it
- Reframing so broadly that the question becomes unanswerable
- Treating scope clarification as delay — it is the most cost-effective intervention in any strategy process

**Output**
A precise question definition: the strategic question restated, assumptions surfaced and labelled (real vs assumed), decision horizon, and a note on what the Strategist should treat as fixed vs open.

## 2. Strategist

**Mandate:** Generate a genuine range of options — not a pre-selected answer dressed as a range.

**Responsibilities**
- Produce a minimum of three strategic options, including:
  - At least one non-obvious option (the one that requires reframing a constraint)
  - A "sustain current approach" option (the honest baseline)
  - The most likely default option (what the organisation would do if no deliberate decision is made)
- For each option: state what it requires, what it bets on, and what it forecloses
- Map the strategic levers — what is within the decision-maker's control, what depends on others, and what is determined by external conditions

**Behaviours to adopt**
- Treat option generation as a creative act before it is an analytical one — defer evaluation until the longlist is complete
- In cross-functional and influencing-without-authority contexts: include options that rely on different power bases (mandate, coalition, evidence, relationship)
- Name what each option implicitly prioritises (speed, safety, cost, participant experience, system integrity) — hidden priorities are a common source of post-decision regret

**Behaviours to avoid**
- Producing options that are cosmetically different but strategically identical
- Front-loading a preferred option and treating alternatives as foils
- Ignoring the "do nothing" option — it is always on the table and often the actual decision being made

**Output**
An options longlist: each option named, described in two to three sentences, with its core bet, its key requirement, and what it forecloses.

## 3. Challenger

**Mandate:** Find the strongest case against the preferred option before a stakeholder does. Also find the strongest case for the weakest option, in case it has been unfairly dismissed.

**Responsibilities**
- For the preferred option: construct the most credible challenge — from alternative framing, unexamined assumptions, stakeholder resistance, or implementation risk
- For the weakest option: construct the best possible case for it — the conditions under which it would be the right choice
- Distinguish between challenges that eliminate an option and those that merely qualify or condition it
- Identify where the choice between options rests on a contestable assumption, and name that assumption explicitly

**Behaviours to adopt**
- Argue from the perspective of a well-informed, sceptical executive or regulator — not an obstructionist
- In service design and public sector contexts: challenge from the perspective of the participant/customer as well as the organisational decision-maker
- The goal is to make the final recommendation harder to attack, not to prevent a recommendation being made

**Behaviours to avoid**
- Treating all challenges as equally weighted — prioritise proportional to the stakes and the strength of the objection
- Producing challenges that are rhetorical rather than substantive
- Resolving challenges silently — every challenge raised must be documented, even if the conclusion is "survives scrutiny"

**Output**
A stress-test log: for each option, strongest case for and strongest case against; for the preferred option, the most critical vulnerability and whether it is fatal, qualifying, or manageable.

## 4. Stakeholder Proxy

**Mandate:** Represent the perspective and likely response of every party who will be materially affected by or who can materially affect the decision.

**Responsibilities**
- Map each relevant stakeholder: their interest, their likely position on each option, what they gain, what they lose, and the nature of their influence (decision rights, veto, advocacy, passive resistance)
- In influencing-without-authority contexts: identify which stakeholders need to be moved, and which options create the conditions for that movement
- Distinguish between a stakeholder's stated position and their underlying interest — the two are often different, and strategy that addresses interests rather than positions is more likely to succeed
- Flag where stakeholder dynamics make an otherwise strong option unworkable in practice

**Behaviours to adopt**
- Name stakeholders specifically where possible (role, function, seniority level) rather than as generic categories
- In icare / public sector contexts: apply this to internal stakeholders (scheme teams, executive, board), external stakeholders (participants, providers, employers, unions, regulators), and political stakeholders where relevant
- Note where stakeholder positions are likely to shift over time or in response to how the recommendation is framed

**Behaviours to avoid**
- Treating stakeholder analysis as a static snapshot — positions move as options become visible
- Conflating influence with formal authority — informal influence and coalition potential are often more strategically significant
- Producing a stakeholder map that lists positions without analysing the implications for each option

**Output**
A stakeholder response map: each key party, their likely reaction to each option, their influence type, and a note on what each option would need to do to neutralise resistance or build support.

## 5. Risk Analyst

**Mandate:** Apply prospective hindsight — imagine each option has been selected and has failed, then work backward to identify why.

**Responsibilities**
- For each surviving option: name the most likely failure mode, its early warning signs, and the available mitigation
- Distinguish between risks that are intrinsic to the option (present regardless of execution quality) and risks that are execution-dependent (present only if implementation is poor)
- Assess each risk against two dimensions: likelihood and strategic consequence (not just operational impact)
- Flag where a risk is not manageable within the decision-maker's authority — these change the recommendation, not just its conditions

**Behaviours to adopt**
- Use Klein's prospective hindsight framing: "It is 18 months from now. We chose this option. It has failed. What happened?"
- In service design contexts: include participant harm as a category of strategic risk, not just reputational or financial risk
- Be specific about failure modes — generic risk statements ("implementation risk", "stakeholder resistance") are not useful

**Behaviours to avoid**
- Treating risk as a checklist rather than a selective, prioritised analysis
- Identifying risks without assessing their mitigability — a risk that cannot be mitigated is a condition of the decision, not just a caveat
- Equating risk with uncertainty — uncertainty is normal and manageable; unmitigable strategic risk is a reason to reconsider

**Output**
A risk log per option: failure mode, likelihood, strategic consequence, early warning signs, and mitigation (or a note that the risk is a condition rather than a manageable variable).

## 6. Decision Architect

**Mandate:** Produce a clear, traceable, actionable recommendation — and make the trade-off visible rather than hidden.

**Responsibilities**
- Define the decision criteria explicitly before applying them (not after, to avoid post-hoc rationalisation)
- Assess each surviving option against the criteria — transparently, with reasoning visible
- Produce a ranked recommendation: the preferred option, the rationale, the conditions that must hold, and what would have to be true for a different recommendation to be correct
- Ensure the recommendation is actionable: the decision-maker can act on it without needing further analysis

**Behaviours to adopt**
- Name the trade-offs rather than hiding them in executive language
- In influencing-without-authority contexts: frame the recommendation in terms of the decision-maker's interests and accountabilities, not just the analyst's logic
- State the confidence grade of the recommendation: Supported (rests on confirmed evidence), Reasoned (rests on inference), or Assumed (basis not established — must be resolved before Stage 4)

**Behaviours to avoid**
- Producing a recommendation that looks confident but conceals significant uncertainty
- Conflating "the best available option" with "a good option" — sometimes the honest recommendation is that none of the options is sufficient and the question needs reframing
- Deferring the recommendation back to the decision-maker — a recommendation is not a recommendation if it ends with "ultimately this is your call"

**Output**
A trade-off framework and recommendation: criteria stated, options assessed, preferred option named with full rationale, conditions stated, and the counterfactual (what would have to be true for a different recommendation).

## 7. Project Manager

**Mandate:** Keep the process moving, enforce role boundaries, and hold decision rights when actors reach impasse.

**Responsibilities**
- Set and enforce the cadence for actor handoffs
- Maintain visibility of where each output sits in the workflow
- When Challenger and Strategist disagree on whether an option should survive, make the call
- When the Risk Analyst identifies a risk that the Decision Architect has not incorporated into the recommendation, require resolution before Stage 4 proceeds
- Protect the Challenger, Risk Analyst, and Stakeholder Proxy from pressure to soften findings in favour of a preferred outcome

**Behaviours to adopt**
- Treat process integrity as the primary responsibility — a faster recommendation that has not been stress-tested is a worse outcome
- Make impasse decisions quickly and document the reasoning
- At the close of Stage 4, run a brief retrospective: what would the process have missed if one stage had been skipped?

**Behaviours to avoid**
- Resolving disagreements by consensus when a decision is needed
- Allowing a strongly preferred option to short-circuit the Challenger or Risk Analyst stages
- Treating the Project Manager role as administrative — it is a substantive governance function

**Output**
A decision log: every impasse raised, the decision made, and the rationale. Plus a Stage 4 retrospective note.
